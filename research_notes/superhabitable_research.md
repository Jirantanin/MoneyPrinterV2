## 1. Origin of the concept — Heller & Armstrong 2014 (source_01)

- Coined the word. Abstract: "We call these objects 'superhabitable'" (the PDF text uses curly quotes). Note: the paper's main body is mostly about **tidal heating making worlds habitable outside the habitable zone**. Superhabitability is the second theme, not the only one.
- Core conclusion (abstract): "they will tend to be slightly older and more massive than Earth and that their host stars will likely be K dwarfs."
- **Earth may be marginally habitable** (Sec. 3): "Earth might well turn out as one that is marginally habitable". Footnote 5 adds: "Earth is located at the very inner margin of the solar habitable zone (Kopparapu et al., 2013)."
- Mass: plate tectonics argument (Sec. 3) — "planets with masses up to about 2 M⊕ tend to be superhabitable from the tectonic point of view." The conclusions widen this: "up to 2 or 3 M⊕, are preferably superhabitable".
- The list of reasons in the conclusions: longer tectonic activity, longer-active carbon-silicate cycle, stronger magnetic shielding, larger surface area, "a smoother surface allowing for more shallow seas", thicker atmospheres.
- Shallow seas (Sec. 3): "Earth's shallow waters have a higher biodiversity than the deep oceans (Gray, 1997)."
- Earth is the largest rocky planet in our Solar System (Sec. 3): "Earth, the only inhabited planet known so far, is the largest terrestrial planet in the Solar System."
- K dwarf lifetimes (conclusions): "These so-called K dwarf stars have lifetimes that are longer than the age of the Universe."
- Proposed target: Alpha Centauri B and its claimed Earth-mass planet "in a 3.235-day orbit (Dumusque et al., 2012)". **Caveat:** that planet (Alpha Cen Bb) was later shown to be almost certainly spurious (source_15: "the 3.24 d signal ... almost certainly arises from the window function"). Do not present Alpha Cen B as hosting a known planet.

## 2. Schulze-Makuch, Heller & Guinan 2020 (source_02)

Bibliographic: *Astrobiology* 20(12):1394–1404, published 14 Dec 2020 (online earlier; WSU release dated 5 Oct 2020). Open access, CC BY 4.0.

### 2a. The criteria (Table 2, "Most Valuable Planets—Planets That Might Be More Habitable Than Earth"), verbatim bullets

1. "In orbit around a K dwarf star"
2. "About 5–8 billion years old"
3. "Up to1.5 more massive than Earth and about 10% larger than Earth" (typo "Up to1.5" is in the original)
4. "Mean surface temperature about 5°C higher than on Earth"
5. "Moist atmosphere with 25–30% O2 levels, the rest mostly inert gases (e.g., N2)"
6. "Scattered land/water distributed with lots of shallow water areas and archipelagos"
7. "Large moon (1–10% of the planetary mass) at moderate distance (10–100 planetary radii)"
8. "Has plate tectonics or similar geological/geochemical recycling mechanism as well as a strong protective geomagnetic field"

Water: no numeric water criterion. The paper says Earth is "already covered by about 71% with water, so it is hard to see how slightly more coverage would be a significant benefit" (Sec. 3.2). The point is land/sea distribution and shallow water, not more water.

Mass reasoning (Sec. 3.2): Heller & Armstrong had allowed up to 2 M⊕, but "many of the exoplanets with two Earth masses are mini-Neptunes" so "a more conservative estimate of up to about 1.5–1.6 Earth masses seems to be in order."

Temperature reasoning (Sec. 3.2): based on the warm, wet early Carboniferous; "a slightly higher temperature, perhaps by 5°C". They admit: "No empirical evidence is available, however, on what that optimum is". Optimum used in the candidate analysis: 19 °C (Sec. 4: "optimal temperature of a superhabitable planet as proposed by us (19°C; Table 3)").

Age reasoning (Sec. 3.2): "a planet with an age of 5–8 billion years should—on statistical grounds—generally be more habitable than Earth."

Testability, in the authors' own words (abstract): "Some of these conditions are far from being observationally testable on planets outside the solar system."

### 2b. How the candidates were picked

- Pool: abstract — "24 top contenders among the >4000 exoplanets known today". Fig. 1 caption: "roughly 4500" planets and candidates, data from exoplanets.org as of 20 May 2019.
- Search box (Sec. 4): habitable zone around K dwarfs taken as "between 0.5 and 1.0 AU and between 0.5 and 1.0 solar masses (Fig. 2)".
- Size cut (Fig. 2 caption): candidates "smaller than 2 Earth radii", although "truly superhabitable planets might be restricted to radii <1.1 Earth radii."
- K dwarf abundance (Sec. 4): K dwarfs are "relatively frequent in the galaxy (~12%), more so than dG stars (~8%)."
- Caveat from the paper itself (Sec. 4): "We caution that we do not have any observational signatures of life from any of these planets."

### 2c. Results — exact numbers (Sec. 4 + Table 3)

- "9 of them are orbiting around K stars, 16 of them are between about 5 and 8 billion years old", and five are within 10 °C of the 19 °C optimum.
- "Only one of the candidates (KOI 5715.01) fits all three criteria, but it has a predicted lower global temperature than Earth". The paper adds that if its greenhouse effect is stronger than Earth's, KOI 5715.01 "could conceivably be superhabitable."
- KOI-456.04 has "the most Earth-like temperature (Heller et al., 2020b)".
- Confirmed versus candidates: "only Kepler 1126 b (KOI 2162) and Kepler-69c (KOI 172.02) are statistically validated planets". The rest "may turn out to be astrophysical false positives."
- Distance: "none of these planets and planet candidates is closer than 100 light years". Kepler-69c: "At a distance of almost 2000 light years, it is simply too far away."
- Mass is unknown for all of them (Sec. 4): "One obvious parameter missing from Table 3 is the planetary mass." Kepler measured transits, which give radius, not mass.

**Press-release discrepancy:** WSU (source_03) says "one has four of the critical characteristics". The paper itself says one candidate fits "all three criteria" (K star, age, temperature). Use the paper's wording.

My cross-checks of the table against the text:
- K-type hosts = 9 ✓. Ages 5–8 Gyr = 16 ✓. Tp′ between 9 and 29 °C = 5 (5819.01, 5554.01, 7894.01, 456.04, 5715.01) ✓. The text's counts match this table.
- **The table has 23 rows, but the text says 24 candidates.** Fig. 2 (an image, not checked) labels "Twenty-four planets and planet candidates". Possibly one Fig. 2 object has no Table 3 row. Unresolved; say "about two dozen" (the paper's own phrase: "at least about two dozen possible candidates") if the exact count matters.
- **Nearest:** KOI 7621.01 at ~365 ly, but its star is only ~0.8 Gyr old. KOI 5554.01 at ~700 ly. The often-quoted "2,965 ly" for KOI 5715.01 checks out (0.909 kpc).
- **Kepler-69c:** the paper says "almost 2000 light years", but its own Table 3 gives 0.746 kpc ≈ 2,430 ly. The NASA Exoplanet Archive today gives 730.6 pc ≈ 2,380 ly. Say "over 2,000 light-years".
- **The headline candidate fails the paper's own size test.** KOI 5715.01's radius range is 1.80–2.39 R⊕. The paper's footnote treats only "<1.6R⊕" as indicating rocky composition. Its Table 2 ideal is ~10% larger than Earth (~1.1 R⊕). Today's KOI table (source_16) gives 1.93 (+0.46/−0.13) R⊕. KOI 5715.01 could easily be a mini-Neptune.
- **Only KOI 5554.01 has its whole radius range below 1.6 R⊕** (0.72–1.29). It is the press's "favourite" (Tp′ ≈ 26 °C, ~700 ly). But it orbits a G1.5V star (Sun-like), not a K dwarf, so it fails criterion 1.

### 2e. Candidate status as of 2026-10-17 (source_16, NASA Exoplanet Archive)

- Kepler-69 c and Kepler-1126 b: CONFIRMED. Radii in the archive's composite table: 1.71 R⊕ and 1.73 R⊕. Both are above 1.6 R⊕.
- The other 20 KOIs that could be queried (5715.01, 5554.01, 5130.01, …) are still **CANDIDATE**. None has been confirmed and none has been reclassified as a false positive.
- Several have low disposition scores (where reported), e.g. 7621.01 = 0.000, 8242.01 = 0.175, 5276.01 = 0.193. A low score means weak confidence that the candidate is a real planet.
- KOI-456.04 (Kepler-160 system) is not in the KOI cumulative table. It came from Heller et al. 2020 (Transit Least Squares). The archive lists Kepler-160 b, c and a non-transiting Kepler-160 d (2020), but not the 456.04 candidate. Treat it as an **unconfirmed candidate**.
- Kepler-69c caveat (source_14): Kane et al. 2013 argued it "is likely a super-Venus rather than a super-Earth". Their figure: incident flux "1.91 times the solar constant". The current archive value is 1.59 times Earth's insolation, which is still well above Earth's.

## 3. Why K dwarfs

- Paper's reasoning (source_02, Sec. 3.1): G stars have "relatively short life spans". It took ~3.5 Gyr for complex life on Earth, so "life on many planets orbiting dG stars may simply run out of time." On M dwarfs it cites tidal locking, loss of seasons, early water loss and "high-energy bursts", and concludes "M dwarfs might not be the best places for superhabitable worlds to emerge." It calls K dwarfs "the most benign (long-lived and stable)".
- Young Sun-like stars (same section): "young dG stars rotate >10 times faster than dG stars near the age of our Sun".
- Cuntz & Guinan 2016 (source_04, abstract): early K stars have "the highest HabPREP values". Red dwarfs' "narrow CLI-HZs and hazards from magnetic activity make them less suitable for hosting exolife."
- NASA "Goldilocks stars" release (source_05, 8 Jan 2020, Guinan & Engle, AAS 235):
  - "These so-called orange dwarfs live from 15 billion to 45 billion years."
  - Sun: "lasts for only 10 billion years."
  - "three times as many K dwarfs in our galaxy as stars like our Sun".
  - "about 1/100th as much deadly X-ray radiation" as planets around active M dwarfs.
  - Red dwarf HZ planets "can be baked bone dry and have their atmospheres stripped away".
- **Lifetime figures disagree.** The WSU 2020 release (source_03) says "lifespans of 20 billion to 70 billion years". NASA 2020 (source_05) says 15–45 billion years. Neither is from a peer-reviewed table I could open. Use "tens of billions of years" (safe), or cite one source explicitly.
- Note: the 2020 paper's frequency figure (~12% K vs ~8% G = 1.5×) differs from NASA's "three times as many". Different counting bases; pick one and cite it.

## 4. The Sun's brightening and Earth's remaining window

Several different end points are measured here, so keep them separate:

| End point | Time from now | Source |
|---|---|---|
| O2-rich atmosphere collapses (O2 > 1% present level ends); driven by CO2 drawdown as the Sun brightens | 1.08 ± 0.14 Gyr | source_07 (press release: "will probably persist for another one billion years (1.08±0.14 (1σ) billion years)") |
| C4 plant CO2-starvation limit (strong weathering case) | 1.35 Gyr | source_09 (abstract: "starvation limit for C4 photosynthesis that occurs at 1.35 Gyr") |
| Too hot for all land plants (>338 K), weak-weathering case | 1.87 Gyr | source_09 |
| 3-D climate model: Earth "may remain 'habitable' for at least another 1.5 billion years", with Sun ~15.5% brighter | ≥1.5 Gyr | source_08 (Wolf, NASA Astrobiology) |
| Earth leaves the habitable zone (simple HZ model) | total HZ lifetime 6.29–7.79 Gyr → ~1.75–3.25 Gyr left | source_06 abstract (total). The "remaining" figure is from press snippets only, not opened |

- Fraction of habitable window used (my arithmetic, not stated in any source): with Earth ≈ 4.5 Gyr old (source_03: "Earth is around 4.5 billion years old") and Rushby's 6.29–7.79 Gyr total, Earth has used about 58–72% of its habitable-zone lifetime. Using the ~1 Gyr biosphere/oxygen limit instead, the share is ~80% or more. Present this as a rough estimate.
- Implied brightening rate: Wolf's +15.5% over ~1.5 Gyr gives roughly 10% per billion years. That figure is derived, not quoted.
- Narration-safe line: "In about a billion years, the brightening Sun will likely end Earth's oxygen-rich era." Avoid "oceans boil in 1 billion years". The ocean-loss/moist-greenhouse threshold comes later in the 3-D models: per source_09 (abstract), these lifetimes "approach" the moist and runaway greenhouse limits.

## 6. Counterpoints and limits

- **Radius valley (source_10, Fulton 2017):** "factor of ≥2 deficit in the occurrence rate distribution at 1.5-2.0 R⊕". Close-in small planets are "rocky cores measuring 1.5 R⊕ or smaller with varying amounts of low-density gas". Caveat: this was measured for P < 100 d. The superhabitable candidates have P ≈ 108–381 d, so applying the valley to them is an extrapolation.
- **Rocky limit (source_11, Rogers 2015):** "the majority of 1.6 Earth-radius planets are too low density to be comprised of Fe and silicates alone." (Measured for P < ~50 d planets.) Most of the 24 candidates' radius ranges extend above 1.6 R⊕. Many may be gas-enveloped mini-Neptunes or water-rich, not super-Earths.
- **Plate tectonics on bigger planets is disputed:**
  - Valencia et al. 2007 (source_12): "super-Earths, even if dry, will exhibit plate tectonic behaviour."
  - O'Neill & Lenardic 2007 (source_13, not opened; from search summary): super-sized Earths are likely in an episodic or stagnant-lid regime.
  - The 2020 paper itself cites the stagnant-lid risk (Stamenković 2012; Noack & Breuer 2014).
  - Treat "longer plate tectonics" as one side of an open debate.
- **Kepler parameter uncertainty:** the radius ranges in Table 3 span roughly 0.5–1 R⊕ (e.g. 1.48–2.64). Ages are "preliminary" (Table 3 note: "Determining stellar ages is a notoriously difficult problem"). No candidate has a measured mass.
- **Selection bias the authors flag (Table 3 note):** "There are biases in this KOI data set that favor older, less photometrically active stars." So the "old age" of the candidates is partly a detection effect.
- **Why we can't look at their atmospheres:**
  - They are ~365 to ~10,000 ly away and faint (Gaia G ≈ 10–16 mag).
  - The paper says even Kepler-69c is "simply too far away" for JWST follow-up.
  - Vilović et al. 2025 (source_21): even for an ideal superhabitable planet at 30 pc (~98 ly), JWST would need "150 transits (43 years)", versus "over 1700 transits" for an Earth twin around a Sun-like star. These durations "lie outside of JWST mission timescales". The Kepler candidates are ~10–100× farther.
- **Future missions (status as of 2026-10-17):**
  - **PLATO (ESA):** launch "planned for March 2027" on Ariane 6 (source_17). This slipped from the earlier "end of 2026" plan. **Not launched as of mid-Oct 2026.** It has 26 cameras and targets "terrestrial exoplanets in orbits up to the habitable zone of Sun-like stars". It finds planets and measures radii (with RV follow-up for masses). It does not measure atmospheres.
  - **ELT (ESO):** "telescope first light" March 2029; "scientific first light" December 2030 (source_18, 14 Mar 2025 schedule).
  - **Habitable Worlds Observatory (NASA):** goal to "identify and directly image 25 potentially habitable worlds" (source_19). The NASA page gives **no launch date**. Secondary sources say the 2040s and report contested funding in the FY2027 budget request; those were not verified from primary documents.
  - Note: the WSU 2020 release names JWST, LUVOIR and PLATO. LUVOIR was folded into HWO.

## 7. Newer work (2020–2026)

- **Olson, Jansen & Abbot 2020, ApJ 895:19 (source_20).** This is the "superhabitable oceans" work, presented at Goldschmidt in **August 2019** (not 2020) and published in ApJ in 2020.
  - Finding: planets "that rotate slower and have higher surface pressure than Earth may be the most attractive targets", because "upwelling is enhanced". It also says "Seasonal deepening of the mixed layer on high obliquity planets may also enhance nutrient replenishment".
  - The abstract does not use the word "superhabitable". The "better than Earth" framing came from the conference press coverage, not opened.
  - Note: this is in tension with Heller & Armstrong's "shallow seas" emphasis only in framing; both favour nutrient-rich surface waters.
- **Vilović, Goyal, Heller & von Schauenburg 2025 (source_21, arXiv preprint).** "planets orbiting mid-type K dwarfs, receiving 80% of Earth's solar flux, are optimal for life". At 60% flux, higher CO2 "could hinder biosphere development". The transit numbers are in §6.
- **Haqq-Misra & Wolf 2026 (source_09):** Earth's own biosphere lifetime (see §4).
- Not found in this pass: a dedicated peer-reviewed critique paper titled around "superhabitability" (2021–2026). The "Do Superhabitable Planets Exist?" piece at reasons.org is an opinion blog, not opened and not a primary source.

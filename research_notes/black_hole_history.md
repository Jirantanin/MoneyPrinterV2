# Black hole history fact-check (1783–1975)

Episode frame: "104 years from Schwarzschild's 1915 solution to the first black hole image in 2019".
Scope: the history part only. Researched 2026-10-19. Every excerpt below was read on the page or in the PDF text listed; excerpts are kept under 15 words.

**Framing note on "104 years":** Schwarzschild's letter with the solution is dated 22 Dec 1915, and Einstein presented the paper on 13 Jan 1916 (HC08, HC11). The EHT image was released on 10 April 2019 (outside this file's scope). 2019 − 1915 = 104 counts calendar years, but only about 103.3 years actually passed. Say "about a hundred years" or "104 years since 1915". Don't say "exactly 104 years".

## Claims

### 1. Michell (1783) and Laplace (1796)

**HC01**: John Michell's paper was a letter to Henry Cavendish. It was read at the Royal Society on **27 November 1783** and printed in *Philosophical Transactions* vol. 74 (dated 1784), pp. 35–57.
- Sources: H02, H01
- Excerpt (H02, header): "Read November 27, 1783."
- Confidence: **verified**
- Don't say "published in 1783". It was *read* in 1783 and appeared in the 1784 volume. APS (H01) cites it as "Vol. 74, p. 35, 1783", which is a common loose citation.

**HC02**: Michell reasoned with Newtonian gravity and the corpuscular theory of light. A body with the Sun's density but 500 times its radius would pull back all the light it emits.
- Sources: H02, H01
- Excerpt (H02, Art. 16): "all light emitted from such a body would be made to return towards it"
- Excerpt (H01, para. on reasoning): "this critical speed would be determined by the mass and size of the star"
- Confidence: **verified**
- Don't say he "predicted black holes" in the modern sense. His idea assumes light particles that slow down. There is no event horizon and no spacetime curvature. Calling them "dark stars" is fine. His real aim was a method for measuring the distances and masses of stars (see the paper's title).

**HC03**: Michell suggested that such invisible bodies might be detected through luminous bodies orbiting them. This anticipates how Cygnus X-1 was actually identified.
- Sources: H02, H01
- Excerpt (H02, Art. 29): "from the motions of these revolving bodies infer the existence of the central ones"
- Confidence: **verified**

**HC04**: Laplace put forward a similar idea of light trapped by very strong gravity in *Exposition du Système du Monde* (1796).
- Source: H01
- Excerpt (H01, Laplace para.): "suggested a similar concept of light being trapped by objects with very high gravity"
- Confidence: **verified** (briefly)
- The often-repeated claim that Laplace dropped the idea from later editions was **not checked**, so don't use it. H01 says the corpuscular theory lost favour after Thomas Young's 1799 experiment showed that light behaves like a wave.

### 2. Karl Schwarzschild (1915–1916)

**HC05**: Schwarzschild, director of the Potsdam Astrophysical Observatory, volunteered for the army when war broke out in 1914. In September 1914 he was sent to Namur, Belgium, as head of a field weather station. Through 1915 he served "as a member of the artillery staff, partly in France and Russia". His war work included ballistics: he published a paper on ballistics in 1916, and MacTutor says he calculated trajectories while in France.
- Sources: H04, H03
- Excerpt (H04, WWI para.): "as a member of the artillery staff, partly in France and Russia"
- Excerpt (H03): "On the outbreak of war in August 1914 Schwarzschild volunteered for military service."
- Confidence: **verified**
- **Myth to avoid:** "he solved Einstein's equations in the trenches". The sources put him on an artillery *staff* on the Eastern Front, not in a front-line trench. "From the Russian front" is safe. "Within range of the guns" is also safe, because his own letter mentions gunfire (HC07). His exact unit and location in December 1915 were not established. One search snippet said he was promoted to Leutnant (Landwehr Fußartillerie) in Sept 1915, but that is **uncertain** and comes from a forum, so don't use it.

**HC06**: On **22 December 1915**, Schwarzschild wrote to Einstein from the Russian front. The letter reworked Einstein's Mercury-perihelion calculation and gave the exact solution, the line element.
- Sources: H06, H05 (§5.1)
- Excerpt (H06, p.5): "On December 22, 1915 Schwarzschild wrote Einstein from the Russian front"
- Confidence: **verified**

**HC07**: The letter closes by saying that, despite heavy gunfire, the war let him take this "walk/stroll in the land of your ideas".
- Sources: H05 (§5.1), H06
- Excerpt (H05/H06 translation): "the war is kindly with me, giving me fire, in spite of fierce gunfire"
- Confidence: the **meaning is verified**, but the **English wording is uncertain**. Every English version is a translation. Weinstein's is awkward. The popular version, "As you see, the war treated me kindly enough, in spite of the heavy gunfire, to allow me to get away from it all and take this walk in the land of your ideas", was seen only in search snippets (Chad Orzel blog, IOP Spark). I could not open the Einstein Papers' authoritative translation (CPAE vol. 8, doc. 169). A search snippet shows the German as "trotz heftigen Geschützfeuers", which is also unverified.
- For the script: paraphrase it in Thai, roughly "despite the heavy gunfire, the war still let him escape for a walk in the land of Einstein's ideas". Don't present any English wording as an exact quote.

**HC08**: Einstein replied on 29 December 1915. He wrote that he would not have expected the exact treatment of the point-mass problem to be so simple.
- Source: H05 (§5.1)
- Excerpt (H05): "the strict treatment of the point-problem was so simple"
- Confidence: **verified** (translation per Weinstein)

**HC09**: Einstein presented Schwarzschild's paper, "Über das Gravitationsfeld eines Massenpunktes nach der Einsteinschen Theorie", to the Prussian Academy on **Thursday 13 January 1916**. It appeared about a month later in the *Sitzungsberichte* (1916, pp. 189–196).
- Sources: H07 (header), H05 (§5.2), H06
- Excerpt (H07): "(Communicated January 13th, 1916 [see above p. 42].)"
- Excerpt (H05): "Einstein delivered Schwarzschild's paper before the Prussian Academy with a few words of explanation"
- Confidence: **verified**
- Schwarzschild couldn't present it himself because he was at the front.

**HC10**: The solution describes the field outside a spherical mass and contains a critical radius α = 2GM/c², now called the Schwarzschild radius. In his second paper (communicated **24 February 1916**), Schwarzschild gave the figures: about 3 km for the Sun and 1.5×10⁻²⁸ cm for one gram.
- Source: H08 (§ "limit to the concentration")
- Excerpt (H08): "For the Sun [α] is equal to 3 km, for a mass of 1 gram"
- Confidence: **verified**

**HC11**: Schwarzschild did **not** treat this radius as something real matter could reach. In his interior (fluid-sphere) paper he concluded that compression has a limit: a sphere of incompressible fluid seen from outside cannot be smaller than 9/8 of α, because the central pressure becomes infinite. In his point-mass paper he chose coordinates that put the "discontinuity" at the origin.
- Sources: H08, H07, H03
- Excerpt (H08): "Hence there is a limit to the concentration"
- Excerpt (H07): "for the discontinuity to go in the origin"
- Confidence: **verified** that his papers set this limit. MacTutor (H03) also characterises the solution as one he considered physically meaningless (paraphrase from the page; no verbatim excerpt captured).
- Don't say "Schwarzschild discovered/predicted black holes" or "he knew stars could collapse into them". Say "his equations contained a boundary that he himself thought nature would never reach".

**HC12**: In Russia he developed **pemphigus**, a painful skin disease that was incurable at the time and is now known to be autoimmune. He was sent home in March 1916 and died in **Potsdam on 11 May 1916**, aged 42 (born 9 Oct 1873).
- Sources: H04, H03
- Excerpt (H04, header): "Died Potsdam, Germany, 11 May 1916"
- Excerpt (H04): "already showed symptoms of pemphigus, a painful and then incurable skin disease"
- Confidence: **verified**
- Don't say he "died in battle" or "died at the front". He died at home of illness, about four months after the solution was presented. He never saw his solution interpreted as a black hole. The term dates from the 1960s, and the first candidate (Cyg X-1) from the 1970s.

### 3. Einstein 1939

**HC13**: Einstein, "On a Stationary System With Spherical Symmetry Consisting of Many Gravitating Masses", *Annals of Mathematics* 40 (Oct. 1939) 922–936, submitted in May 1939. He modelled a swarm of particles in circular orbits and found that they cannot be packed tightly enough for the "Schwarzschild singularity" to form, because the orbital speeds would have to reach the speed of light. He concluded that the essential result was understanding why "Schwarzschild singularities" do not exist in physical reality.
- Sources: H09, H10 (§ on Einstein 1939)
- Excerpt (H09, quoting Einstein): "why the "Schwarzschild singularities" do not exist in physical reality"
- Excerpt (H10): "worked out how a swarm of particles would behave as they collapsed"
- Confidence: **verified**
- In 1939, "Schwarzschild singularity" meant the surface r = 2m, what we now call the horizon, not the central singularity (H09). His model was a *static* cluster, so it says nothing against *dynamic* collapse. That is exactly what Oppenheimer & Snyder treated the same year. According to Bergmann (per H10), Einstein was not aware of Oppenheimer's papers. Don't say "Einstein proved black holes impossible". Say "Einstein argued that nature would never let it happen".

### 4. Chandrasekhar and Eddington

**HC14**: Chandrasekhar was born on 19 Oct 1910 in Lahore. He left Bombay on **31 July 1930**, aged 19, aboard the Lloyd Triestino steamer *Pilsna* bound for Venice, and went on to Cambridge on a Government of India scholarship.
- Sources: H10 (§ on Chandra's voyage), H13, H11, H12
- Excerpt (H10): "he left Bombay on the steamer Pilsna, a liner of Lloyd Triestino"
- Excerpt (H13): "departed Bombay on July 31, 1930, bound for Venice."
- Confidence: **verified**
- H13's text, as fetched, gave the birth date as 13 Oct. NobelPrize.org (H11, H12) gives **19 October 1910**, so use the Nobel date.

**HC15**: On board, he worked out the statistical mechanics of the degenerate electron gas in white dwarfs. He realised that the electrons must be treated relativistically, which implies a maximum mass. The voyage figure was about **0.91 M☉**, using the then-assumed molecular weight 2.5. With the later value of 2.0 it became about **1.44 M☉**, the "Chandrasekhar limit" (≈1.4 M☉).
- Sources: H13, H10, H14, H15
- Excerpt (H13): "spent his time on shipboard working out the statistical mechanics"
- Excerpt (H14): "0.91 times the mass of the Sun"
- Confidence: **verified**
- **Myth to avoid:** "he calculated 1.4 solar masses on the ship". The figure on the ship was lower, and 1.44 came later. "On the voyage he found that white dwarfs have an upper mass limit" is safe. Stoner and Anderson reached related results independently (H14), so don't call him the *only* person to think of it.

**HC16**: At the Royal Astronomical Society meeting on **Friday 11 January 1935** (Burlington House, London), Chandrasekhar presented his results. Eddington, whom he had not known was going to speak on the topic, followed immediately with his own paper, "Relativistic Degeneracy", attacking the result. It was printed in *The Observatory* 58 (1935) 37–39.
- Sources: H16, H10, H17, H14
- Excerpt (H16, timeline): "January 11, 1935 Battle with Eddington at the RAS"
- Confidence: **verified**

**HC17**: Eddington's words, as printed in *The Observatory* 58, p. 38, and quoted in H10. He described a star contracting "until, I suppose, it gets down to a few kilometres radius when gravity becomes strong enough to hold the radiation and the star can at last find peace". He called the result "almost a reductio ad absurdum of the relativistic degeneracy formula" and ended: "I think that there should be a law of nature to prevent the star from behaving in this absurd way".
- Sources: H10 (quoting Eddington 1935a), H17, H14
- Excerpt (H10): "a law of nature to prevent the star from behaving in this absurd way"
- Excerpt (H10): "the star can at last find peace"
- Confidence: **verified** via the scholarly quotation of the primary source. I could not open the scan of *The Observatory* itself.
- The popular version, "...prevent **a** star from behaving in this absurd way!" (H17, H14), differs slightly. Use the H10/Observatory wording. Note that Eddington essentially described a black hole forming and then rejected it. The "stellar buffoonery" line comes from a **1936 Harvard lecture**, not the 1935 meeting (H14), so don't put it in the 1935 scene.

**HC18**: The effect on Chandrasekhar. Parker's NAS memoir says the episode pushed him "farther into the obscure and lonely byways of science" and that the "Eddington factor" closed the doors in England. He finished his stellar-structure work with a 1939 book and then moved to other fields. He described his career as seven distinct periods. He arrived at Yerkes Observatory (University of Chicago) on 21 Dec 1936 and formally joined the faculty in January 1937. He stayed at Chicago for the rest of his life and died there on 21 Aug 1995.
- Sources: H13, H11, H14, H12
- Excerpt (H13): "served to push Chandra farther into the obscure and lonely byways of science"
- Excerpt (H13): "The Eddington factor had the effect of closing the doors in England"
- Excerpt (H11): "I joined the faculty of the University of Chicago in January 1937."
- Excerpt (H14): "he decided to move on to other fields of study"
- Confidence: **verified**
- Don't say he "fled England the next day". The Chicago offer came during a Harvard visit in Jan–Mar 1936 (H11), and he kept defending the work until the 1939 book. They also remained friends: H11 lists Eddington among his "lasting friendships", and the ODNB entry (H31) notes Eddington helped get him elected to the Royal Society in 1944. One search summary mentions a 2010 *Annals of Science* paper arguing that the community's reception of his work was more favourable than legend says. I **did not open it**, so treat the "everyone sided with Eddington" framing with care.

**HC19**: He won the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1983, shared half-and-half with William A. Fowler, "for his theoretical studies of the physical processes of importance to the structure and evolution of the stars". His last research period was *the mathematical theory of black holes* (1974–1983), and the book of that title appeared in 1983.
- Sources: H12, H11, H13
- Excerpt (H12): "physical processes of importance to the structure and evolution of the stars"
- Excerpt (H13): "in recognition of his work of fifty years before"
- Excerpt (H11): "There have been seven such periods in my life"
- Confidence: **verified**

### 5. Oppenheimer & Snyder 1939

**HC20**: J. R. Oppenheimer and H. Snyder (Berkeley), "On Continued Gravitational Contraction", *Phys. Rev.* 56, 455 (vol. 56, issue 5). It was **received 10 July 1939** and **published 1 September 1939**. A distant observer sees the star approach its gravitational radius only asymptotically, with its light increasingly reddened. For an observer moving with the matter, the collapse (with pressure neglected) takes a finite time, about a day.
- Sources: H18, H10
- Excerpt (H18, abstract): "When all thermonuclear sources of energy are exhausted a sufficiently heavy star will collapse."
- Confidence: **verified**
- The title is "Continued", not "Continual".

**HC21**: Its publication date, 1 September 1939, is the day Germany invaded Poland and the Second World War in Europe began.
- Sources: H18, H19
- Excerpt (H19): "On the morning of 1 September 1939, Hitler's voice was heard on the radio."
- Confidence: **verified** as a date coincidence (the journal issue date).
- Don't claim the war "buried" the paper as an established single cause. The fact that Oppenheimer went on to the Manhattan Project is safe. A search result says the same issue also carried Bohr & Wheeler's fission paper (pp. 426–450). That is **uncertain** because I did not open the page, so verify it before using.

### 6. The name "black hole"

**HC22**: The first known appearance of "black hole" in print is Ann Ewing's report in *Science News Letter*, **18 January 1964**, on an AAAS meeting in Cleveland. The line reads: "Such a star then forms a 'black hole' in the universe." She did not say which speaker used the phrase.
- Sources: H20, H21, H22
- Excerpt (H20): "Such a star then forms a “black hole” in the universe."
- Excerpt (H21): "which appeared in a story by Ann Ewing in the January 18, 1964, issue."
- Confidence: **verified**
- The date of the Cleveland meeting is **uncertain** in my sources. H22 says December 1963, while H21's wording suggests January 1964. A *Physics Today* notice seen only in a search snippet gives 26–30 Dec 1963. Don't give a date for the meeting.

**HC23**: The phrase had already been spoken at the first Texas Symposium in Dallas in **December 1963**, and it appeared in *Life* on **24 January 1964**. Hong-Yee Chiu, the Cleveland organiser, said he did not coin it and traced it to Robert Dicke's lectures around 1960–62, which compared collapsed stars to the "Black Hole of Calcutta".
- Sources: H21, H22
- Excerpt (H21): "She discovered that 'black hole' was spoken in Dallas in December 1963."
- Confidence: Dallas and *Life* are **verified**. Dicke as the originator is **uncertain**: it rests on recollections, and H22's own conclusion is interpretive.
- H22 wrongly dates the Dallas symposium to "July 1963". It was December 1963, per H21.

**HC24**: John Wheeler popularised the term. He used it in his Sigma Xi–Phi Beta Kappa lecture at the AAAS meeting in the New York Hilton on **29 December 1967**, and it was printed in 1968 ("Our Universe: The Known and the Unknown", *American Scientist* 56 and *The American Scholar* 37). His story that an audience member at a Goddard Institute conference in the autumn of 1967 suggested "How about black hole?" is disputed: the pulsar conference he described took place in May 1968.
- Sources: H22, H21
- Excerpt (H22): "on December 29th 1967, Wheeler was invited to give a talk"
- Excerpt (H22): "the conference on pulsars and neutron stars at the Goddard Institute, only"
- Confidence: the 1967 lecture and the 1968 print are **verified**. The audience anecdote is **uncertain**.
- **Myth:** "Wheeler coined 'black hole' in 1967". Better: "Wheeler made the name famous in 1967–68, but it had appeared in print in 1964 and nobody knows who said it first." According to H21, Wheeler himself never claimed he invented it.

### 7. Cygnus X-1 and the Hawking–Thorne bet

**HC25**: Cygnus X-1 was first detected in **1964** by Geiger counters on a Naval Research Laboratory **Aerobee sounding rocket** launched from White Sands, New Mexico. It was published as Bowyer et al. 1965, *Science* 147, 394.
- Source: H23 (§3)
- Excerpt (H23): "in an NRL Aerobee launch from White Sands in New Mexico in 1964"
- Confidence: **verified**
- It is fine to say "discovered in 1964". The 1965 date is the publication year. I could not open the Bowyer paper itself.

**HC26**: The Uhuru satellite was launched in December 1970. Its observations, published in 1971 (Schreier et al.), showed that Cyg X-1's X-ray brightness flickers on timescales down to about 0.1 s, which means the emitting region is tiny. Better positions from Uhuru and an MIT rocket led to a radio counterpart.
- Source: H23
- Excerpt (H23): "fluctuations in the X-ray intensity on timescales down to 100 ms"
- Confidence: **verified**

**HC27**: In 1972 two teams independently identified the optical counterpart, the blue supergiant HDE 226868. Louise Webster and Paul Murdin (Royal Greenwich Observatory) published in *Nature* 235, 37 (received 17 Nov 1971; published 7 Jan 1972; title ends with a question mark). C. T. Bolton (David Dunlap Observatory, Toronto) published in *Nature* 235, 271 (published 4 Feb 1972). Both found that the star's radial velocity varies with a 5.6-day orbit, so it is being pulled around by an unseen companion.
- Sources: H24, H25, H23
- Excerpt (H24): "whose changes of velocity correlate with changes in the X-ray flux"
- Excerpt (H25): "varies with a period consistent with that of the X-ray variation"
- Confidence: **verified**

**HC28**: The mass argument: the orbit makes the unseen companion far heavier than the maximum mass of a neutron star, so it is a black hole candidate. In H23's retrospective summary, the companion is about 15 M☉.
- Source: H23
- Excerpt (H23): "much larger than the maximum value expected for a neutron star"
- Confidence: the argument is **verified**. The 1972 numbers are **uncertain**: the 1972 papers' own mass estimates were lower and hedged, and 15 M☉ is a later figure.
- Don't say the 1972 papers "proved" a black hole. H23 says it became "widely – though not universally" accepted.

**HC29**: The bet document, in Kip Thorne's handwriting, is dated **10 December 1974**. Hawking bet that Cygnus X-1 was *not* a black hole. If it was proven to be one, Hawking would give Thorne a 1-year subscription to *Penthouse*. If it was proven not to be one, Thorne would give Hawking a 4-year subscription to *Private Eye*. Hawking called the bet his "insurance policy".
- Sources: H27, H28, H26, H30
- Excerpt (H27): "Bet on the existence of a Black Hole at Cygnus X-1. 10 December 1974."
- Excerpt (H28, quoting *A Brief History of Time*): "This was a form of insurance policy for me."
- Confidence: **verified**
- **Commonly misreported:** Hawking's own *A Brief History of Time* says 1975, and many sites copy that. The document says 1974. The exact written wording, reportedly "does not contain a black hole of mass above the Chandrasekhar limit", appeared only in search snippets, so it is **uncertain** and shouldn't be quoted. For a Thai family audience, describe the stakes as "a magazine subscription" if needed.

**HC30**: Hawking conceded in **June 1990**. While Thorne was away (Thorne says in Moscow, per a search snippet), Hawking and colleagues got into Thorne's Caltech office, took out the framed bet, and signed his concession with a thumbprint.
- Source: H26 (p. 138)
- Excerpt (H26): "In June 1990 Hawking decided that the evidence was now overwhelming, and paid up."
- Confidence: **verified** (the "Moscow" detail is **uncertain**)

### 8. Hawking radiation, 1974

**HC31**: S. W. Hawking, "Black hole explosions?", *Nature* 248, 30–31, received 17 Jan 1974 and **published 1 March 1974**. Once quantum effects are included, a black hole should emit particles as if it were a hot body, with a temperature inversely proportional to its mass. It therefore slowly loses mass. Any primordial black hole lighter than about 10¹⁵ g would already have evaporated, ending in a burst equal to about a million 1-megaton bombs.
- Source: H29 (opening paragraph)
- Excerpt (H29): "any black hole will create and emit particles such as neutrinos or photons"
- Excerpt (H29): "Any such black hole of mass less than 10^15 g would have evaporated by now."
- Confidence: **verified**
- The title really has a question mark. For a solar-mass black hole the evaporation time is far longer than the age of the universe, so don't imply that real black holes are exploding now. Hawking radiation has **not** been observed from any astrophysical black hole.

## Story beats worth using

1. **Dark stars (1783).** John Michell writes to Cavendish that a star big enough would pull its own light back. He adds that you could still find it by watching what orbits it. Two centuries later, that is exactly how Cygnus X-1 was caught. (HC02, HC03, HC27)
2. **A letter from the guns (Dec 1915).** An observatory director serving on an artillery staff on the Russian front sends Einstein the first exact solution of general relativity. He closes by saying that, despite the gunfire, the war let him take a walk "in the land of your ideas". Paraphrase it; the exact English wording varies by translation. (HC05, HC06, HC07)
3. **Einstein reads it for him (13 Jan 1916).** Schwarzschild is still at the front, so Einstein stands up in the Prussian Academy and presents a paper for a colleague who can't be there. (HC09)
4. **Four months.** He comes home with a skin disease nobody could treat and dies in Potsdam on 11 May 1916, aged 42. His own papers said that matter could never actually be squeezed inside the radius that now carries his name. (HC10, HC11, HC12)
5. **Nineteen and at sea (Jul–Aug 1930).** On the steamer *Pilsna* from Bombay to Venice, a 19-year-old with a scholarship works out that white dwarfs can't be heavier than a certain limit. (HC14, HC15)
6. **The star "can at last find peace" (11 Jan 1935).** Minutes after Chandrasekhar speaks, the most famous astronomer in Britain describes, in words, a star collapsing until gravity traps its light, and then calls it absurd. "There should be a law of nature to prevent the star from behaving in this absurd way." (HC16, HC17)
7. **The long way round.** Chandrasekhar leaves for Chicago, changes fields again and again, and four decades later writes *The Mathematical Theory of Black Holes* (1983). That same year he receives the Nobel Prize for work done fifty years before. (HC18, HC19)
8. **Same day, two catastrophes (1 Sept 1939).** The paper showing that a heavy star collapses without end is published the morning the Second World War begins. Its first author would soon be building the atomic bomb. (HC20, HC21)
9. **Einstein says no (1939).** Months earlier the same year, Einstein publishes his argument that the "Schwarzschild singularities" do not exist in physical reality. (HC13)
10. **Nobody knows who named it.** "Black hole" appears in print in a 1964 science-news report whose author never names the speaker. Wheeler, who made it famous in 1967, never claimed he invented it. (HC22, HC23, HC24)
11. **The insurance bet (10 Dec 1974).** The man who did the most to understand black holes bets that the best candidate *isn't* one, so that if he was wrong about his life's work he'd at least win a magazine subscription. Sixteen years later he breaks into Thorne's office and signs the concession with his thumbprint. (HC29, HC30)
12. **The black hole that glows (1974).** In a two-page letter with a question mark in its title, Hawking shows that black holes aren't completely black. (HC31)

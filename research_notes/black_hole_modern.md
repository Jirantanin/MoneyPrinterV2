## Claims

### A. Stars around Sgr A* (Ghez and Genzel) and the 2020 Nobel

**MC01: The 2020 Nobel was split: half to Roger Penrose, half jointly to Genzel and Ghez.**
Sources: M01. Excerpt: "black hole formation is a robust prediction of the general theory of relativity" (Penrose citation). Locator: prize announcement, top of press release. Confidence: verified.
Do not say: that Penrose "discovered" or "proved the existence of" real black holes. His work is mathematical: black holes can form under realistic conditions in general relativity and always hide a singularity.

**MC02: Genzel and Ghez were cited for "a supermassive compact object", not for "a black hole".**
Sources: M01. Excerpt: "for the discovery of a supermassive compact object at the centre of our galaxy". Also: "A supermassive black hole is the only currently known explanation." Locator: press release, citation plus second paragraph. Confidence: verified.
Note: the Nobel wording is careful. Narration can say "the only known explanation is a black hole" but should not call the stars "proof" in the strict sense.

**MC03: Two independent teams have watched these stars since the early 1990s. Genzel's group used ESO telescopes in Chile (NTT, then VLT). Ghez's group used Keck on Mauna Kea.**
Sources: M01, M02, M03, M05, M06. Excerpts: M01 "since the early 1990s, has focused on a region called Sagittarius A*". M03 "a 26-year-long observation campaign". M02 "For almost thirty years, Reinhard Genzel and Andrea Ghez have followed their stars". Locators: M01 para 3; M03 lead; M02 section on adaptive optics. Confidence: verified.
Notes: Genzel/ESO count 26 years up to 2018. The UCLA data in M05 start in 1995 ("measurements from 1995-2017"). Use "about a quarter century" or "nearly 30 years", not an exact single number for both teams.

**MC04: The central mass is about 4 million solar masses, packed into a region no larger than the Solar System.**
Sources: M01, M03, M20. Excerpts: M01 "packed together in a region no larger than our solar system". M03 "a mass four million times that of the Sun". Confidence: verified.

**MC05: S2 (Ghez's team calls it S0-2) orbits in about 16 years, short enough to follow a full orbit.**
Sources: M02, M04, M05. Excerpts: M02 "completes an orbit of the centre of the galaxy in less than 16 years". M04 "16-year-period orbit of the star S2". Confidence: verified.
Comparison usable from M02: the Sun takes more than 200 million years per lap of the galaxy ("dinosaurs were walking the Earth when we started our current lap").

**MC06: Closest approach (pericentre) was in May 2018, about 120 AU from Sgr A* (under 20 billion km), at about 7,650 km/s. ESO rounds this to over 25 million km/h, or almost 3% of light speed.**
Sources: M03, M04. Excerpts: M04 "Near pericentre at 120 AU, ~1400 Schwarzschild radii". M04 "the star has an orbital speed of ~7650 km/s". M03 "less than 20 billion kilometres from the black hole". M03 "almost three percent of the speed of light". Confidence: verified.
Notes: 7,650 km/s is about 2.55% of c, about 27.5 million km/h. Say "almost 3%" or "over 25 million km/h". Do not say "3% of light speed" flat.

**MC07: GRAVITY on ESO's VLT detected S2's gravitational redshift at the 2018 pericentre (published 26 July 2018). The measured effect matched general relativity and ruled out pure Newtonian gravity.**
Sources: M03, M04. Excerpts: M03 "The new measurements clearly reveal an effect called gravitational redshift." M04 "The S2 data are inconsistent with pure Newtonian dynamics." Value: f = 0.90 ± 0.09 (stat) ± 0.15 (sys), where f = 1 means GR. Confidence: verified.
Notes: Strictly, the measured shift is gravitational redshift combined with the relativistic transverse Doppler effect. "Gravitational redshift" alone is what ESO says and is fine for narration. Ghez's UCLA team confirmed it independently with Keck. Their paper reports Υ = 0.88 ± 0.17 and excludes Newton at 5σ (M05; arXiv July 2019, Science). Credit both teams, or say the GRAVITY team was first.

**MC08: The 2018 pass was the second pericentre Genzel's team had observed. The first was in 2002, which follows from the 16-year period.**
Sources: M03. Excerpt: "the second time that we have observed the close passage of S2" (Genzel quote). Confidence: verified (the year 2002 is inferred from the period, not quoted).

**MC09: Distance to Sgr A* is about 26,000–27,000 light-years.**
Sources: M03 ("26 000 light-years"), M20 ("about 27 000 light-years"). Confidence: verified. Use about 27,000, the later ESO figure.

**MC10: Penrose's key paper was published in January 1965. He introduced "trapped surfaces" and showed that a black hole always hides a singularity. The idea came to him in autumn 1964 while crossing a street in London.**
Sources: M01, M02. Excerpts: M01 "In January 1965, ten years after Einstein's death, Roger Penrose proved". M02 "an idea flashed into his mind" (while stopping to cross a side street). Confidence: verified (M02 attributes it to "as he later recalled").

### B. Gravitational waves: GW150914

**MC11: Both LIGO detectors recorded the signal on 14 September 2015 at 09:50:45 UTC. The announcement came on 11 February 2016.**
Sources: M07, M08. Excerpts: M08 "On September 14, 2015 at 09:50:45 UTC the two detectors". M07 press release dated "February 11, 2016" and gives "5:51 a.m. Eastern Daylight Time (09:51 UTC)". Confidence: verified.

**MC12: The masses were about 36 and 29 solar masses, merging into about 62. About 3 solar masses of energy were radiated as gravitational waves.**
Sources: M08, M07, M10. Excerpt: M08 "the initial black hole masses are 36 ... and 29". M07 "About 3 times the mass of the sun was converted into gravitational waves". Confidence: verified.
Note: 36 + 29 = 65, minus 62 = 3. This works well as an on-screen subtraction.

**MC13: The distance is about 1.3 billion light-years (luminosity distance 410 Mpc, +160/−180).**
Sources: M08, M09, M10. Excerpts: M10 "1.3 billion lightyears from Earth". M09 "It took 1.3 billion years for the waves to arrive". Confidence: verified. M10 adds that this was when life on Earth was going from single-celled to multicellular.

**MC14: The signal lasted about 0.2 s in band, rising over about 8 cycles from 35 Hz to 150 Hz, where amplitude peaked. That rising pitch is the "chirp".**
Sources: M08, M11. Excerpt: M08 "Over 0.2 s, the signal increases in frequency and amplitude in about 8 cycles". Locator: PRL Section II. Confidence: verified.
Note: the abstract says the signal "sweeps upwards in frequency from 35 to 250 Hz". So 150 Hz is where amplitude peaks and 250 Hz is the top of the sweep. Pick one and keep it consistent, or skip Hz numbers.

**MC15: Livingston recorded the event 7 milliseconds before Hanford. The waves travel at light speed, and the two sites are about 3,000 km apart.**
Sources: M07, M10. Excerpt: M07 "the detector in Livingston recorded the event 7 milliseconds before". Confidence: verified.

**MC16: The peak power was about 50 times that of the whole visible universe.**
Sources: M07. Excerpt: "a peak power output about 50 times that of the whole visible universe". Confidence: verified.
Do NOT say "50 times more energy". The LIGO video caption (M11) words it as energy, which is wrong. It is peak power, and only for a fraction of a second.

**MC17: Einstein predicted gravitational waves in 1916 and doubted they could ever be measured. For years he was even unsure they were physically real.**
Sources: M08, M09, M10, M07. Excerpts: M08 "In 1916, the year after the final formulation of the field equations". M09 "Einstein was convinced it would never be possible to measure them." M07 quotes Bruce Allen: "Einstein thought gravitational waves were too weak to detect". Confidence: verified.

**MC18: The 2017 Nobel went to Rainer Weiss (half), and jointly to Barry Barish and Kip Thorne.**
Sources: M09. Excerpt: "for decisive contributions to the LIGO detector and the observation of gravitational waves". Confidence: verified.

**MC19: The official audio exists. In raw form the signal sounds like a faint thump. LIGO and GWOSC also publish versions shifted up 400 Hz so the chirp is audible.**
Sources: M11, M12. Excerpts: M12 "They sound like faint thumps; it's hard to hear the 'chirp'." M12 "Gravitational waves are not sound". Confidence: verified.
Do not say "we heard the black holes" literally. The audio is a conversion of detector data. If you play the shifted version, label it as pitched up.

**MC20: The first person to notice the signal was Marco Drago, a young physicist at the Max Planck Institute for Gravitational Physics in Hannover. It was 11:51 local time and he was getting ready for lunch, while the US was asleep. The team could not tell anyone, even their families, until February 2016.**
Sources: M10. Excerpts: "Marco Drago, a young physicist at the Max Planck Institute". "allowed to reveal the news to anyone, even their families". Confidence: verified (Nobel popular science text).
Note: M10 also says it might have been one of the secret "blind injection" test signals. It was not.

### C. Event Horizon Telescope: M87* (2019)

**MC21: The image was released 10 April 2019 at coordinated press conferences worldwide, with six papers in ApJL.**
Sources: M14, M15, M16. Excerpt: "in coordinated press conferences across the globe". Confidence: verified.

**MC22: M87* was observed on 5, 6, 10 and 11 April 2017.**
Sources: M16. Excerpt: "We observed M87* on 2017 April 5, 6, 10, and 11 with the EHT." Locator: Paper I, Section 4. Confidence: verified.
Note: the whole 2017 campaign ran 5 nights (M18). M87* data came from 4 of them.

**MC23: There were eight telescopes at six geographic locations: volcanoes in Hawai'i and Mexico, mountains in Arizona and Spain's Sierra Nevada, the Atacama Desert in Chile, and Antarctica.**
Sources: M14, M15, M16. Excerpts: M16 "eight stations over six geographical locations". M15 "volcanoes in Hawai`i and Mexico, mountains in Arizona". Confidence: verified.
Telescopes: ALMA, APEX, IRAM 30 m, JCMT, LMT, SMA, SMT, SPT.

**MC24: The telescopes form an "Earth-sized virtual telescope" by VLBI at a 1.3 mm wavelength. The official resolution analogy is reading a newspaper in New York from a (sidewalk) café in Paris.**
Sources: M14, M15, M18. Excerpts: M15 "enough to read a newspaper in New York from a sidewalk café in Paris". M14 omits "sidewalk". The angular resolution is 20 micro-arcseconds. Confidence: verified.
Notes: "orange on the Moon" was NOT found in the official materials checked. The NAOJ fact sheet (M18) uses a different analogy: "the size of a tennis ball in the distance of the moon". Use the newspaper/Paris one. The telescopes are not physically connected: hydrogen-maser atomic clocks time-stamp the data (M14 note 3).

**MC25: M87* has about 6.5 billion solar masses and is about 55 million light-years away.**
Sources: M14, M15, M16. Excerpts: M14 "resides 55 million light-years from Earth". M16 "M = (6.5+/-0.7) x 10^9 Msun". Confidence: verified.

**MC26: The image shows the black hole's shadow (a dark central region) surrounded by a bright, lopsided emission ring of hot gas, with light bent by gravity. It is not a picture of the hole itself.**
Sources: M14, M15, M16, M20. Excerpts: M15 note 1 "the closest we can come to an image of the black hole itself". M14 "a ring-like structure with a dark central region — the black hole's shadow". M16 "asymmetric bright emission ring with a diameter of 42+/-3 micro-as". Confidence: verified.
Do NOT say:
- "a photograph of a black hole": it is a radio-wave (1.3 mm) image reconstructed computationally from interferometer data;
- that the dark disc is the event horizon: M15 says the horizon is "around 2.5 times smaller than the shadow it casts";
- that the orange colour is real: it is a false-colour rendering of radio brightness.

The brighter bottom part of the ring comes from relativistic beaming of plasma moving near light speed (M16).

**MC27: Each telescope recorded about 350 TB per day onto helium-filled hard drives. The drives were physically flown to two correlator supercomputers, at MPIfR Bonn and MIT Haystack.**
Sources: M14, M15, M17, M18. Excerpts: M15 "roughly 350 terabytes per day". M14 "These data were flown to highly specialised supercomputers". M18 "approximately 3500 TB of raw data recorded to disks". Confidence: verified.
Notes: the official fact sheet gives about 3.5 PB for the 2017 campaign. The popular "5 petabytes / half a ton of drives" figure appears only in press quotes (Dan Marrone, via Inverse), not in any official doc opened. Say "petabytes" or "about 3.5 petabytes", or mark 5 PB as a quoted estimate. The fact that disks were shipped because that was faster than the network is supported by M19.

**MC28: South Pole data had to wait out the polar winter. The station has no flights from February to October, so the April 2017 disks sat in "cold storage". They left in early November and went via McMurdo, Christchurch and Port Hueneme. They reached MIT Haystack on 13 December 2017.**
Sources: M19, M17. Excerpts: M19 "no flights in or out from February to October". M19 "the long-awaited shipment of hard disk drives from the South Pole finally arrived". M17: SPT observing is run by "a two-person SPT winter-over team". Confidence: verified (EHT blog via Wayback).
Notes: the South Pole Telescope doubled the north–south extent of the array, and therefore the resolution (M18).

**MC29: The collaboration had more than 200 researchers and 13 stakeholder institutes. ALMA, with 66 antennas, was the most sensitive station.**
Sources: M14, M18. Excerpt: M14 "a team of more than 200 researchers". Confidence: verified. By 2025 the collaboration listed more than 400 researchers (M22).

### D. Sgr A* image (2022)

**MC30: The Sgr A* image was released 12 May 2022. It came from 2017 data and shows a shadow and ring similar to M87*'s.**
Sources: M20. Excerpt: "a dark central region (called a shadow) surrounded by a bright ring-like structure". Confidence: verified.
Usable size analogy (official): "about the same size in the sky as a doughnut on the Moon".

**MC31: Sgr A* was harder to image even though it is closer. It is about 1,000 times smaller, so gas orbits it in minutes, compared with days to weeks for M87*. The published image is an average of many images.**
Sources: M20. Excerpts: "in the much smaller Sgr A* it completes an orbit in mere minutes". "a bit like trying to take a clear picture of a puppy". "an average of the different images the team extracted". Confidence: verified. (Quote is from EHT scientist Chi-kwan Chan.)

### E. Follow-ups (2024–2025), one or two lines each

**MC32: M87* in 2018 data (published 18 January 2024): the ring is still there, about the same size (43.3 µas), but its brightest part moved about 30°. In January 2025 the team attributed the shift to turbulence in the accretion flow.**
Sources: M21, M22. Excerpts: M21 "The diameter of the 2018 ring is remarkably consistent". M21 "shifted by about 30 relative to 2017" (degrees). M22 "the brightest region of the ring has shifted 30 degrees counter-clockwise". Confidence: verified.

**MC33: Sgr A* in polarised light (27 March 2024) shows strong, organised, spiralling magnetic fields similar to M87*'s.**
Sources: M24. Excerpt: "strong, twisted, and organised magnetic fields near the black hole". Confidence: verified.

**MC34: M87* polarisation flips (16 September 2025): the magnetic-field pattern spiralled one way in 2017, settled in 2018 and reversed in 2021, while the ring size stayed the same. The same work made the first EHT detection of faint emission at the jet base.**
Sources: M23. Excerpt: "the polarization pattern unexpectedly flipped direction". Confidence: verified.

### F. Information paradox (current status)

**MC35: Hawking, Thorne and Preskill made a public bet in 1997. Hawking and Thorne bet that information is lost in black holes; Preskill bet it is not. Hawking conceded in Dublin in 2004 and paid with a baseball encyclopedia. Thorne did not join the concession.**
Sources: M25 (primary, Preskill himself), M26 (dates). Excerpts: M25 "before an audience in Dublin of 700 scientists and at least 50 reporters". M25 "Total Baseball: The Ultimate Baseball Encyclopedia". Confidence: concession, Dublin and the prize are verified (M25). The signing date of 6 February 1997, the concession date of 21 July 2004 and Thorne not conceding are **uncertain**: they come from Wikipedia only, because the Caltech bet page was blocked.

**MC36: Status: since 2019–2020, semiclassical "island"/Page-curve calculations indicate information can get out of an evaporating black hole. Quanta called the paradox "near its end". The mechanism is still not mapped and not everyone accepts these calculations, so the paradox is not fully settled.**
Sources: M27. Excerpts: "this is at most the end of the beginning". "Theorists still haven't mapped the step-by-step process". "And not everyone is convinced." Confidence: verified (for the 2020 snapshot).
Suggested one-paragraph summary: In 1974–76 Hawking showed that black holes slowly radiate and evaporate, and argued that whatever fell in is erased. That would break a basic rule of quantum mechanics. In 1997 he bet on information loss. In 2004 he conceded that information escapes, though not everyone accepted his argument. Around 2019–2020 several groups calculated that the entropy of Hawking radiation follows the "Page curve", meaning information does get out. Quanta reported in 2020 that the paradox was nearing its end. Physicists still do not know how the information actually escapes, and some remain unconvinced. So say "physics' most famous open question is close to an answer, but not finished", not "solved" and not "completely mysterious".
Do not say: "unresolved, no progress", or "Hawking proved information is lost".

### G. Flags for other parts of the script

**MC37: Schwarzschild's solution was published in 1916.** The PRL (M08) says: "Also in 1916, Schwarzschild published a solution for the field equations". The solution was worked out in late 1915 (letter to Einstein, December 1915), but that date was not verified here. If the hook says "1915 → 2019 = 104 years", phrase it as "found at the end of 1915". Check this in the historical research notes. Confidence: uncertain (1915 derivation date not checked here).

**MC38: Hawking's Cygnus X-1 bet was placed in 1974.** Source: M32: "Stephen Hawking lost a bet — originally placed in 1974". Confidence: verified. The page's "about 15 times the mass of the Sun" is a 2011 figure; do not quote it without a newer source.

## Story beats worth using

1. **Lunch in Hannover (MC20, MC11).** At 11:51 local time in Hannover, while America slept, Marco Drago was about to go to lunch. A curve appeared on his screen that matched the shape he had practised spotting. His first worry was that it might be a secret test signal. It was not, and the team then had to keep it from their own families for five months.
2. **0.2 seconds after 1.3 billion years (MC13, MC14, MC12).** The two black holes circled each other for millions of years. The part that reached us lasted about 0.2 seconds and 8 wave cycles. In that time 3 Suns' worth of mass vanished into gravitational waves. Visual: 36 + 29 = 65 → 62.
3. **The thump vs the chirp (MC19).** Play the raw GWOSC audio, which is a faint thump, then the +400 Hz version, where the chirp is audible. Say plainly that gravitational waves are not sound.
4. **Einstein's doubt (MC17).** He predicted the waves in 1916 and thought nobody would ever measure them. Bruce Allen's line (M07): Einstein "didn't believe in black holes. But I don't think he'd have minded being wrong!" Paraphrase it; do not quote at length.
5. **One star, 26 years, one appointment in May 2018 (MC03, MC05–MC08).** A team waits 16 years for the same star to swing back past the same invisible object. Genzel had seen the 2002 pass with weaker instruments. In 2018 GRAVITY could see the star move night to night (ESO eso1825e diagram) at nearly 3% of light speed. Its light came back reddened by exactly the amount Einstein's theory predicts.
6. **The dinosaur comparison (MC05).** S2 laps the centre in under 16 years. The Sun's last lap began when dinosaurs walked the Earth (M02).
7. **Penrose crossing the street (MC10).** In autumn 1964 an idea came to him mid-conversation while crossing a London side street. He only recovered it later that afternoon. That idea, the trapped surface, became the 1965 proof.
8. **Disks frozen at the South Pole (MC28, MC27).** The South Pole data were recorded in April 2017 by a two-person winter-over crew. No plane could land until spring, so the drives sat in "cold storage". They travelled by air, sea and land via McMurdo and Christchurch, and reached Haystack by FedEx on 13 December 2017. The EHT's own caption says there is no faster way to move petabytes than putting disks on planes.
9. **Reading a newspaper in New York from Paris (MC24).** This is the official analogy for 20 micro-arcsecond resolution. It pairs well with a globe showing the 8 sites.
10. **A shadow, not a photo (MC26).** Contrast what people think they saw with what it actually is. The EHT's own words: the shadow is "the closest we can come to an image of the black hole itself". The true horizon is about 2.5 times smaller than the dark patch.
11. **The puppy chasing its tail (MC31).** Gas around our own galaxy's black hole goes round in minutes, so the image changed while the shutter was "open". The team had to average many images.
12. **The ring is still there (MC32, MC34).** A year later the ring was the same size but its bright spot had turned about 30°. By 2021 the magnetic pattern had reversed. The black hole is real and permanent; its surroundings are weather.
13. **A bet paid in baseball (MC35, MC36).** Hawking conceded in front of 700 scientists in Dublin and handed over a baseball encyclopedia. He later joked he should have given Preskill the ashes; that line is from Wikipedia, so treat it as uncertain. Close with the honest status: nearly answered, not finished.
